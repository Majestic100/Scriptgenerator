# Schwartz's copy techniques (reference)

Select techniques according to the missing belief, the state of awareness and the market's sophistication. Never let a formula replace analysis of the product, market and timing.

## Seven central processes

| Process | Function | Modern use |
|---|---|---|
| Intensification | Presents the dominant outcome through new, desire-building perspectives | Demonstration, use-case scene, first-day story, before/after, expert reaction, comparison or guarantee |
| Identification | Connects the product with a credible desired role, character, status or self-expression | Creator selection, segment situation, brand role and "for people like me" cues |
| Gradualization | Builds from accepted facts towards a claim that would otherwise meet resistance | Known observation → diagnosis → mechanism → claim → proof → CTA |
| Redefinition | Changes the perspective on difficulty, importance or price | Smaller first step, broader value, total-cost comparison or realistic consequence |
| Mechanization | Explains why and how the product can deliver the claim | Named mechanism, process demonstration, product design, method or reason why |
| Concentration | Focuses on a genuine weakness in the standard alternative and proves the product removes it | Fair side-by-side, trade-off analysis and method-specific comparison |
| Camouflage | Adapts expression to the familiar form of the medium | Native creator format and channel language, clearly disclosed as advertising |

**Ethical translation:** Do not use these techniques to fabricate problems, hide total cost, imitate editorial content without disclosure, construct straw-man competitors or invent proof. Claims must be supportable, comparisons fair and native advertising identifiable.

## Gradualization steps

| Step | Prospect reaction | Copy function |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Accepted reality | "Yes, I experience that." | A precise situation, symptom or desired state |
| 2 · Interpretation | "That explains something." | A cause, pattern or frame that follows from accepted reality |
| 3 · Mechanism | "I can see how it could work." | A concrete reason why and relevant limitations |
| 4 · Claim | "That outcome is now plausible." | One primary promise rather than many partially believed promises |
| 5 · Proof and action | "It is supported enough for the next step." | Evidence at the point of doubt and a stage-appropriate CTA |

## Redefinition: the three classic objections

| Objection | Redefinition | Responsible application |
|---|---|---|
| "It is too difficult" | Change the perceived task and make the first step smaller | Show the real process, time and work required |
| "It is not important enough" | Broaden the role, frequency or larger consequence | Connect only to realistic outcomes; do not manufacture fear |
| "It is too expensive" | Change the comparison to total value, useful life, alternative cost or lost outcome | Show total price, assumptions and trade-offs; do not hide continuing costs |

## Stage × process

| Stage | Processes likely to matter | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Identification, gradualization, camouflage and mood | Create recognition and bridge from the familiar world to a new meaning |
| Problem Aware | Intensification, gradualization and redefinition | Clarify the problem, make hope plausible and reduce perceived difficulty |
| Solution Aware | Mechanization, concentration and verification | Provide decision criteria and a credible explanation of the difference |
| Product Aware | Verification, concentration, intensification and redefinition | Resolve fit, proof, risk and value objections |
| Most Aware | Redefinition, verification and concentrated intensification | Remove the last price, timing or friction barrier without re-educating |

## 38 headline and hook levers (expression library — not a substitute for research)

1. Quantify the size of the result. 2. Quantify speed or time. 3. Compare with a familiar alternative. 4. Use a precise metaphor. 5. Make the result sensory. 6. Demonstrate with one powerful example. 7. Dramatise the completed result. 8. Use a truthful paradox. 9. Remove a known limitation. 10. Associate with a desired value, group or role. 11. Specify the scope of the work. 12. Ask the decisive question. 13. Offer concrete how-to information. 14. Use relevant authority. 15. Show before and after on the same basis. 16. Feature what is genuinely new. 17. Show legitimate exclusivity. 18. Invite a test or challenge. 19. Use a specific case statement. 20. Show what the product replaces or consolidates. 21. Use a parallel situation as a symbol. 22. Connect mechanism directly to claim. 23. Surprise with an unexpected but truthful mechanism. 24. Connect need and result in one statement. 25. Open with useful information or relevant curiosity. 26. Turn the claim into a short case history. 27. Name the problem or need precisely. 28. Warn about a real pitfall. 29. Use meaningful rhythm or repetition. 30. Show ease through a simple prerequisite. 31. State the difference concretely. 32. Show which former limitation has been removed. 33. Define who the product is not for. 34. Address the relevant person or situation directly. 35. Show the effort or quality behind the product. 36. Acknowledge that the claim may sound too good, then prove it. 37. Challenge a limiting assumption. 38. Use question and answer to resolve the central doubt.

**Use rule:** Select mass desire, awareness, sophistication and belief shift first. Then use one to three verbalisation levers to sharpen the idea. If the lever pushes the claim beyond what can be documented, reduce the claim; do not decorate the proof.
